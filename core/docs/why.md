# Foundation

## Inception

Knowledge is personal and bounded. There is no universal interpretation — only people holding beliefs shaped by their experience, bounded by what they care about.

We continually gather and review our beliefs through cognitive work and learning. From before birth, a person absorbs experience — sensation, language, instruction, reflection, contradictions, evidence, distinctions and negations. This is not passive recording. Every piece of new information meets existing beliefs and gets interpreted, connected and abstracted. Over a lifetime, this produces a vast personal understanding: a living structure of how a person believes things relate.

**Abstract begins with a practical question: how can we give this evolving understanding an explicit form that people and machines can work with together?**

Much of a person's understanding remains implicit. We recognize situations, follow conventions, and communicate through language whose meaning depends on shared experience. This allows us to think and interact without explaining every assumption. It also makes our understanding difficult to examine, communicate, and combine with somebody else’s.

We need a way to express understanding while it is still taking shape: to begin with a few meaningful assertions, inspect their consequences, introduce further distinctions, and revise our understanding as we learn.

## A language and its basic unit

Abstract seeks the precise minimum for this work — a small structure that makes interpretations explicit without requiring a comprehensive description of the world.

A **judgment** is the basic unit of the language. It expresses a person's knowledge claim about how a relationship is understood. Writing it explicitly makes the claim available for examination; it does not establish that the claim is true.

People distinguish things and recognize connections between them. They understand those connections through relationships they already know. A judgment makes one such interpretation explicit: it states that one relationship is, or is not, an abstraction of another.

A judgment's simple structure gives people and AI agents a shared object to work on. A person and an agent can inspect assertions, propose additions, identify conflicts, and revise relationships. Their recorded judgments remain available beyond the conversation and can be interpreted together as a graph.

Explicit graph structure gives machines something they can process and people something they can question.

### Why subjectivity demands communication

Because each person develops their understanding through their own experience, knowledge is irreducibly subjective. No two people interpret the same experience identically. Yet we live in a shared reality and need aligned understanding to act together. This creates a natural pressure to communicate — to share beliefs, expose contradictions, negotiate meaning, and reach consensus. Consensus does not dissolve subjectivity; it creates shared knowledge layered on top of personal belief, while each person's understanding remains their own.

Communication does not transfer understanding intact from one mind to another. Another person interprets what we express through their own experience and conceptual distinctions.

An explicit graph gives us something shared to examine. We can ask which relationships have been asserted, what abstractions give them meaning, and where our interpretations differ. Agreement can become more specific, and disagreement can become easier to locate.

This also creates a basis for composition. Bringing graphs together involves examining how their concepts and relationships correspond. Matching names alone cannot establish shared meaning.

The aim is to make enough of our interpretation explicit that people can work on their understanding together.

## Purpose and limitations

Abstract is intended to support the continuing work of understanding: capturing relationships, expressing their interpretations, communicating them, and revising them as knowledge develops.

Making understanding explicit does not make it complete. A graph's internal consistency does not guarantee that it describes the world correctly. Questions of evidence, authorship, uncertainty, and justification remain important; the basic judgment form does not answer them automatically.

The purpose of Abstract is to give developing understanding a form that is small enough to author, precise enough to inspect, and structured enough to share and build upon.
